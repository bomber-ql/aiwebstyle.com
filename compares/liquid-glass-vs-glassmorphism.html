Liquid Glass vs Glassmorphism

These two names get used interchangeably, and on a screenshot they nearly are. The difference appears when you look at where each one puts its depth: one builds it into the border, the other hangs it outside the card.

Short answer: Glassmorphism floats — a large soft outer shadow, a hover lift, and a light streak that fades at both ends. Liquid Glass sits — a hard uniform top highlight, a heavier border, and no shadow at all.

Both panels load the same page — only the stylesheet differs.

What actually separates them

Both styles are the same three ingredients: a saturated field behind the page, translucent panels above it, and backdrop-filter to blur what is behind. That is why they read as siblings and why most comparisons end in a shrug.

The divergence is in the edge. In this gallery the Glassmorphism card draws its top light with a ::before element using a horizontal gradient that fades out at both ends, and then lifts the card 4px on hover above a 50px soft shadow. The Liquid Glass card does neither: it paints a single uniform line with box-shadow: inset 0 1px 0 at 55 percent, keeps the card still, and relies on a 28 percent border to separate it from the field.

Decision table

A filled dot marks the side that handles the dimension more convincingly. It is a judgement, not a rule — several rows are genuinely a tie.

Dimension Liquid Glass Glassmorphism
Top edge light One uniform 1px inset line at 55% white, full width ● A gradient streak that fades to transparent at both ends
Depth cue Inside the border — the panel reads as thick material ● Outside the card — a 50px soft shadow, so it floats
Border weight ● 1px at 28% white 1px at 22% white
Card radius 24px 20px
Blur on cards blur(24px) saturate(170%) blur(20px) saturate(150%)
Hover Background lightens, card stays put ● Card lifts 4px, border brightens to 34%

The CSS that changes

These are the declarations that make the difference visible. Everything else — font sizes, spacing, section order — is shared, because both pages use the same markup.

Property Liquid Glass Glassmorphism
Top light box-shadow: inset 0 1px 0 rgba(255,255,255,.55) ::before { height:1px; background: linear-gradient(90deg, transparent, rgba(255,255,255,.75), transparent) }
Outer depth none box-shadow: 0 20px 50px -30px rgba(0,0,0,.9)
Border 1px solid rgba(255,255,255,.28) 1px solid rgba(255,255,255,.22)
Hover background: rgba(255,255,255,.14) transform: translateY(-4px); border-color: rgba(255,255,255,.34)

When each one wins

Liquid Glass wins when

  • The panel is large and sits over a photographic or video background, where a soft shadow would smear into the image
  • You want the card to read as a physical slab — a panel of frosted acrylic rather than a floating sheet
  • The page has many stacked panels; hover lifts on every one become a nervous, fidgety surface

Glassmorphism wins when

  • The card is small and needs to separate from a busy field — the soft shadow does that work at a glance
  • You want a hover affordance on a grid of cards, where the lift reads as clickable
  • The background is dark and flat, so a 50px shadow has somewhere to fall and does not look like dirt

Steering a model to one or the other

The words that steer a model between these are specific: ask for a single inset top highlight with no drop shadow and no hover transform, and you get Liquid Glass. Ask for a gradient streak on the top edge plus a large soft shadow and a 4px hover lift, and you get Glassmorphism. Naming the material rather than the style — slab versus pane — gets you there faster than naming the style at all, because both names are ambiguous enough that a model will pick either.
