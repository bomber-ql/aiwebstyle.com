Aurora UI vs Liquid Glass

Both are dark pages with colour bleeding through translucent panels. One animates the light source; the other treats the panel as the material.

Short answer: Aurora moves the background: soft colour fields drift behind static glass. Liquid Glass holds the background still and puts the work into the panel edge — a hard inset highlight and a heavier border, with no shadow at all.

Both panels load the same page — only the stylesheet differs.

What actually separates them

Aurora UI is a background technique. The panels barely change — a 1px border at 14 to 18 percent white and a modest blur — while the colour behind them drifts on 14 to 22 second cycles. Switch the animation off and the page still reads as aurora, because the colour field is the identity.

Liquid Glass is a surface technique. The field behind is two strong radial gradients pinned to the viewport, and it does not move. All the character is in the card: a 28 percent border, a 1px inset highlight at 55 percent along the top edge, and deliberately no drop shadow, so the panel reads as a slab rather than a floating pane.

Decision table

A filled dot marks the side that handles the dimension more convincingly. It is a judgement, not a rule — several rows are genuinely a tie.

Dimension Aurora UI Liquid Glass
What moves Three gradient fields, 14 to 22s cycles Nothing
Panel edge 1px at 14 to 18% white ● 1px at 28% plus an inset top highlight
Background #0a0d18 with drifting radials #0b1020 with two fixed radials at 50% scrim
Drop shadow None None
Radius 16px 24px
Failure mode Motion that becomes the subject A flat field leaves the highlight with nothing to catch

When each one wins

Aurora UI wins when

  • The page is a hero or a landing section where atmosphere is the point and reading is brief
  • You want the site to feel alive without any interaction
  • The content sits in one short block rather than a long scroll

Liquid Glass wins when

  • Panels are the interface — cards, modals, sidebars — and each needs a clear edge
  • You want the look without any animation, which makes it far easier to keep accessible
  • The background is a photograph or a video rather than a gradient field

Steering a model to one or the other

Ask for drifting radial gradients and you get Aurora; ask for an inset top highlight, a 28 percent border and no shadow and you get Liquid Glass. The two are often generated together by accident — a model asked for glassmorphism with a fancy background will produce both, and the result is a moving field under panels that also have drop shadows, which is three depth cues fighting. Pick one: motion behind static glass, or a static field with material panels.
