Blog / Editorial Site

Most AI-generated blogs are a list of cards with a stock photograph each, and an article page whose text runs the full width of a laptop screen. The list is fine and the article is unreadable. Line length is the whole game.

Content 8 sections 5 variables 8 min read

What this page has to do

Two numbers decide whether an editorial site works. The first is line length: between 60 and 72 characters. Browser defaults produce 120 to 140 on a desktop, and at that width the eye loses its place on the return sweep. The second is the ratio of index to article: the index exists to send someone into a piece, so every card needs a reason to be clicked other than a picture.

There is also a structural problem specific to AI output. Asked for a blog, a model produces an index and omits the article template entirely, or produces an article template with no support for code blocks, footnotes, pull quotes or captions — the four things that immediately break a reading column when they arrive.

Section order

The order is the argument. Reordering these is not a layout decision, it is a change to the reasoning.

  1. 01

    Masthead

    Publication name, one line of what it covers and for whom, and a subscribe field. Three elements, no hero image.

  2. 02

    Featured

    One piece, chosen for depth rather than recency. A blog that leads with its newest post has no editorial judgement; a blog that leads with its best does.

  3. 03

    Index

    Date, title, one-line summary, reading time. Newest first, with the occasional older piece pinned when it is more useful than what is on top.

  4. 04

    Article template

    A fixed reading column at 66 to 70 characters, with the title allowed to break out slightly wider. This is where the site is actually read.

  5. 05

    Rich content rules

    Code blocks, footnotes, pull quotes, captions and tables all need a defined width and behaviour, or they break the column the moment an author uses one.

  6. 06

    Author block

    One sentence and a link, placed after the piece rather than before it. Readers decide whether to trust the writing from the writing.

  7. 07

    Related

    Three pieces, chosen by topic overlap rather than by tag equality. Tag-based related lists are usually empty or wrong.

  8. 08

    Archive and feed

    A plain chronological archive and a real RSS link. Both are how a body of work becomes findable after the first month.

Build the prompt

5 variables. Change one and the text below rewrites itself — nothing else on this page moves.

The most readable option and the hardest to make look empty.

Best for essays over 1,500 words.

Assembled prompt 
Build an editorial site for a publication of long-form essays. Pieces run 2,000 to 4,000 words, publish twice a month, and are argued rather than reported. The index should feel sparse and deliberate..

AUDIENCE
Readers who arrive from a search result or a shared link, read one piece, and decide from that piece whether to subscribe. They are not browsing a brand; they are testing whether the writing is worth their time.

STRUCTURE
1. Masthead: publication name at 2rem, one line naming the subject and the reader, a subscribe input with a working success state. No hero image, no carousel.
2. Featured piece: exactly one, chosen for depth. Title, deck of two sentences, author, date, reading time, and a 16:9 image only if a real one exists.
3. Index: Render the index as text rows: date in a 90px left column, then title at 1.25rem with the summary beneath, reading time right-aligned in mono. No images at all. Each entry shows date, title, a one-sentence summary, and reading time. Newest first, with an optional pinned entry marked as such.
4. Article template: Set body copy in a serif at 19px with 1.68 line height and a 68 character measure; headings in a sans at 600 weight. Serif at this size reads faster for long pieces. Title may extend to 1.15 times the column width; body stays inside it. Every paragraph 2 to 4 sentences.
5. Rich content rules: code blocks scroll horizontally and never widen the column; footnotes appear as numbered superscripts linking to a list at the end; pull quotes indent 1.5rem and use the display face at 1.35rem; captions sit at 0.8rem in muted text directly under the figure; tables scroll horizontally on narrow viewports.
6. Author block: one sentence in first person and one link, placed after the final paragraph.
7. Related: exactly three pieces chosen by topic overlap, each with its reading time.
8. Archive and feed: a chronological archive page linked from the footer, plus a visible RSS link.

STYLE
Editorial: warm paper background at #fbf9f5, near-black text, one thin rule under the masthead, no cards, no shadows, dates in small caps with 0.06em tracking.

MOTION
No animation anywhere. Editorial sites date fastest when they move, and the reader came for the text.

COPY RULES
- No clickbait headlines, no questions as headlines, no numbers in a title unless the piece actually contains that many items.
- Every index entry needs a summary that states the argument, not the topic. A piece about caching says what it concludes about caching.
- Dates always shown as a full date, not as 3 days ago.
- Reading time computed at 220 words per minute, rounded up.
- No author bio longer than two sentences. No social sharing bar above the article.

CONSTRAINTS
- Body line length between 60 and 72 characters at every viewport above 900px.
- Font size 18 to 20px for body text, line height 1.6 to 1.7.
- Heading hierarchy: one h1 per page, h2 for sections, h3 for subsections. Never skip a level.
- No layout shift from images: width and height on every figure.
- The article must be readable with JavaScript disabled, including footnotes.

OUTPUT
Three page types sharing one stylesheet: index, article, archive.

What to change, and what to leave alone

VariableHow to edit it
{{PUBLICATION}} This changes the index density and the metadata, not just the copy. An engineering notebook needs revision dates and status; a reporting outlet needs datelines and sections. Keeping the same template for both is why the result feels generic.
{{LIST}} Text rows are the honest choice when you have three posts. Cards with images need images for every entry — one card without a picture breaks the grid visibly.
{{READING}} Choose by length, not by taste. Serif at 19px rewards a 3,000-word essay and punishes a 300-word changelog entry, where sans at 18px reads faster.
{{STYLE}} Swap the block whole. A literary brief applied to half the page produces a drop cap on the index and sans headings in the article.
{{MOTION}} None is a defensible choice, not a lazy one. Say so explicitly, or a model will add scroll reveals to every paragraph.

Feeding it to each builder

BuilderWhat to do differently
v0 Generate the article page first. It is the page that gets read, and every index decision should follow from it. Index-first generation produces beautiful lists over unreadable articles.
Lovable It will add a reading progress bar. Decide deliberately: a progress bar is useful on a 4,000-word essay and noise on a 400-word note.
Bolt Ask for the three page types in one project so the stylesheet is shared. Separate generations drift, and the archive ends up with a different column width than the article.
Cursor / Claude Code Write one real article as a fixture first, including a code block, a footnote and a quote, then ask for the template. Designing against real content prevents the four breakages listed above.

Where it usually breaks

01A 140-character line

The single most common defect, and the one that matters most. A full-width paragraph on a 1440px screen is roughly 140 characters, which is twice the comfortable maximum. The fix is one property: max-width around 68ch on the body column.

02No dates

Readers use dates to judge relevance, and search engines use them for freshness. A blog without visible dates reads as abandoned, even when it published this morning.

03Tag soup

Twenty tags with two posts each is a navigation system that helps nobody. Either keep five categories and no tags, or use tags that each cover at least six pieces.

04Rich content that breaks the column

A code block, a wide table or a full-bleed image will widen the page and destroy the reading measure unless each has an explicit width rule. These four rules belong in the prompt, not in a later bug report.
